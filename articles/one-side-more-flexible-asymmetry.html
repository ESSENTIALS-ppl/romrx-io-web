One Side More Flexible Than the Other: What It Means

By Jim Scott, Founder of ROMRx · Published July 24, 2026 · Updated July 24, 2026

Having one side more flexible than the other is common and usually normal. Left-right differences in range mostly reflect hand or leg dominance and daily habits rather than a problem. Measuring the gap, then giving the tighter side extra targeted volume (and unilateral strengthening where relevant) usually narrows it, while a large, painful, or newly appeared asymmetry deserves a closer look.

Why having one side more flexible is often normal

Perfect left-right symmetry is rare. Individual range varies and often differs from population norms, so a modest side-to-side difference is expected. Symmetry is a rough goal, not a mandate.

Common causes

Hand and leg dominance, the side you habitually sit or sleep on, sport-specific patterns (a lead leg or throwing arm), and past minor injuries all nudge one side toward more or less range. These are ordinary explanations, not alarms.

How to measure your side-to-side difference

Measure the same joint on both sides with a consistent method. On the weight-bearing lunge test, for example, a difference greater than about 2 to 3 cm is worth noting, especially if the tighter side also has symptoms.

How to train the tighter side

Give the tighter side extra volume and, where relevant, unilateral strengthening. Training through range, including single-side work, builds usable capacity on the weaker side. Practical steps:

  • Start and finish sets with the tighter side.
  • Add an extra set or two of mobility for that side.
  • Use single-limb strength work through full range.

When asymmetry is worth a closer look

Red flags. A sudden, painful, or large new asymmetry, or asymmetry with weakness, numbness, or swelling, should be assessed by a clinician rather than aggressively stretched.
Nuance. Small differences are normal; you do not need to chase perfect balance to be healthy or to train well.

Frequently asked questions

Is it normal for one side to be more flexible?

Yes. Modest left-right differences are common and usually reflect dominance and daily habits. Individual range varies, so some asymmetry is expected rather than a problem.

What causes left-right flexibility differences?

Hand and leg dominance, habitual postures, sport-specific patterns, and past minor injuries. These ordinary factors nudge one side toward more or less range.

Should I only stretch the tight side?

Give the tighter side extra targeted volume rather than ignoring the other side entirely. Adding unilateral strength through range on the tighter side also helps.

How big a difference is a problem?

Small differences are normal. A large, painful, or newly appeared asymmetry, or one with weakness, numbness, or swelling, is worth having assessed.

Can lifting cause asymmetry?

A dominant side or uneven technique can create modest differences over time. Unilateral work and starting sets with the weaker side help keep the gap small.
